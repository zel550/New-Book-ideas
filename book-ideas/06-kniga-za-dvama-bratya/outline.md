# Книга за двама братя (Two Brothers) — Full Outline

## Logline
When their grandmother is given days to live, a young man flies to London to physically drag his estranged, furious older brother home to Bulgaria — and the reluctant road trip across England and Europe becomes the only conversation the two of them have avoided having for a decade.

## Genre
Literary Fiction / Road Trip Drama — Contemporary, in the vein of *Death of a Superhero*, *The Trip* (for its brother-banter texture), and *A Man Called Ove* for its blend of comedy and grief.

## Premise Expansion
The brothers haven't spoken properly in years. Bogomil ("Bogi"), the elder, left Bulgaria for London young and has spent years turning private shame and self-directed rage into distance — cutting off calls, missing holidays, letting the family believe he simply doesn't care. Todor ("Tosho"), the younger, stayed close to home, built a steady life, and has spent those same years quietly terrified of losing anyone else the way they've already lost their parents (implied earlier family tragedy — a car accident, a death, something that split the family's sense of safety when they were kids). When their grandmother — the one person who never let either of them go — is dying, Tosho refuses to let Bogi miss it, shows up unannounced at his door, and half-drags him into a cross-continental journey home: London to Bulgaria, overland, because Bogi's passport situation / a canceled flight / a superstition ("she deserves the actual journey, not a plane ticket") forces them onto the road together.

## Themes
- Anger as inherited grief that never got named
- The difference between running from a family and running from yourself
- Siblings as the only witnesses left to a shared childhood
- Reaching someone in time — and what "in time" really means when it's not just about the deathbed
- Home as a place you have to choose to return to, not just a place you're from

## Principal Characters
- **Todor "Tosho" Ivanov** — late 20s, the younger brother and our primary POV. Responsible almost to a fault; his fear of loss shows up as control — overplanning the trip, over-managing Bogi, unable to sit with uncertainty.
- **Bogomil "Bogi" Ivanov** — early 30s, the older brother. Left for London ostensibly for work, actually to escape a version of himself he can't forgive (implied: a mistake tied to their parents' accident, or a breakdown he's never explained to the family). Sharp, defensive, funny in a way that's mostly armor.
- **Baba Rada** — their grandmother, dying, mostly present through phone calls, memories, and letters/flashbacks; the emotional gravity well the whole book orbits.
- **Elena** — a woman the brothers meet on the road (a fellow traveler, a innkeeper, a stranger who joins part of the journey) who becomes an outside mirror for both brothers' unspoken issues; optional light romantic thread with Tosho or simply a catalytic outside voice.
- **Their parents (deceased, via flashback)** — the source of the unspoken family wound; specifics to be developed, but should tie directly to why Bogi's anger is self-directed and why Tosho's fear centers on losing family without warning.

## Structure: Three Acts, 24 Chapters

### Act One — The Door in London (Ch. 1–6)
1. Tosho gets the call: Baba Rada has days, maybe a couple of weeks. He immediately starts planning to bring Bogi home, over their mother's/aunt's doubt that Bogi will even come.
2. London. Tosho shows up at Bogi's flat unannounced — the "What's going on, psycho!" line lands here, establishing their prickly, love-under-armor dynamic immediately.
3. Bogi refuses, deflects, picks a fight; we see the shape of his self-loathing without yet knowing its cause.
4. Tosho reveals the real ultimatum — he's not leaving without him, even if it means missing his own flight, losing his job for the week, sleeping on Bogi's floor.
5. A complication strands them on the ground route rather than flying (a lost passport, a grounded flight, a superstitious promise to Baba Rada, or Bogi's refusal to fly for a reason tied to their parents' accident) — the road trip is now the only option, and the clock is now brutally literal.
6. They set off — first hours in the car/train are pure friction: old grievances surface fast, both performing indifference.

### Act Two — The Road (Ch. 7–18)
7. First stop across the Channel/into mainland Europe — a logistics mishap forces actual cooperation; first crack of real teamwork since childhood.
8. Flashback interlude: a specific, warm childhood memory (the two of them, Baba Rada, before the family wound) — establishes what they're trying, without saying so, to get back to.
9. An argument boils over into something real — Tosho finally names what he's scared of: that Bogi disappearing to London was Bogi choosing to be lost to them, the same as their parents were lost.
10. Bogi, cornered, gives a first partial truth about why he left — not the full story yet, but enough to reframe his anger as guilt rather than coldness.
11. They meet Elena at a stop along the way — her outside perspective (a stranger who's lost someone too, or who calls out their dynamic bluntly) gives both brothers language they can't yet use with each other.
12. A logistical crisis (car trouble, missed connection, a border/document issue) forces a delay, raising the stakes on the clock to reach Baba Rada in time — and forcing more hours together than either wants.
13. Bogi's anger flares outward for the first time at someone other than Tosho or himself (a stranger, a service worker) — Tosho sees, for the first time, how much rage Bogi's been carrying and how little of it has anywhere to go.
14. Quiet chapter: the brothers, stuck somewhere overnight, actually talk — games from childhood, an old joke, the first real laughter of the trip.
15. The full truth about the family wound surfaces (recommend: their parents died in an accident when the brothers were teenagers; Bogi, driving or present in some capacity, has carried private guilt he's never spoken aloud, believing the family blames him even though they don't — or worse, half-believing they should).
16. Tosho's own fear is reframed by hearing this: he realizes his controlling, over-managing behavior on the trip has been about not losing Bogi the way he believes he "lost" him once already, silently, for years.
17. A near-miss (an accident, a scare, something road-trip-real) forces both brothers to physically protect each other — a plot beat that makes the reconciliation kinetic, not just verbal.
18. Word arrives that Baba Rada has taken a turn — the journey's back half becomes a genuine race, urgency overriding old caution.

### Act Three — Home (Ch. 19–24)
19. Final leg into Bulgaria — the landscape becomes emotionally loaded (last time either of them were both here together, the funeral of their parents, childhood places).
20. A last obstacle threatens to make them too late (weather, a final logistics failure, or an internal one — Bogi freezing up, unable to face walking back into that house).
21. Tosho, finally letting go of control, doesn't manage or push Bogi through it — instead sits with him in the fear, the book's thesis landing in action rather than speech.
22. They arrive — whether in time or by minutes/hours too late is the book's key craft decision. Recommended: they arrive in time, but barely, and the scene is not a clean, cinematic goodbye — it's messy, quiet, real (Baba Rada lucid only in flashes, both brothers present regardless).
23. Aftermath at the family home — extended family, funeral or vigil, the brothers now visibly different with each other than they were in Chapter 2; old family friction with aunts/mother resolved or at least aired.
24. Coda: some months later, back to ordinary life — Bogi hasn't "fixed" himself completely (recommend against a too-clean redemption), but the brothers are in real contact now; final image ties back to the opening door-knock, inverted — Bogi shows up at Tosho's door this time, unannounced, using their private joke.

## Craft Notes
- Keep the road-trip incidents grounded and specific (real countries, real logistics) rather than generic "travel montage" — texture is what will sell the intimacy.
- Resist a full-forgiveness ending for Bogi's guilt; grief and self-directed anger don't resolve on a deadline, only get witnessed and shared, which is the more honest ending.
- Alternate close-third POV between the brothers by act if a fuller emotional picture is wanted (Act 1 Tosho-heavy, Act 2 split evenly, Act 3 Bogi-heavy) — or keep it single-POV (Tosho) throughout for tighter unity; recommend single-POV for a debut-scale manuscript.
