# Ideas for a Script — Three Short Film Treatments

This source document is a screenwriting notebook, not one story — three unrelated short-film concepts (roughly 5–20 minutes each), closer to festival short-film pitches than a novel or feature. Each is outlined separately below as its own treatment. If the author wants to pursue one, it should be developed independently of the others.

---

## Treatment 1: "The Things We Couldn't Say" (working title, alt: "...If We Could" / "...If We Would")

### Logline
A couple's relationship curdles from soulmate-euphoria to silent resentment over the accumulation of small daily grievances — and in the ten seconds before she walks out the door for good, we watch the raw, honest conversation they'll never actually have.

### Genre
Short-form romantic drama, dialogue/visually driven, single-location-heavy. Comps per the source notes: *500 Days of Summer*, the Netflix anthology series *Modern Love* / *Love*, and the "unspoken conversation" device used in films like *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind* and *La La Land*'s epilogue.

### Structure (roughly 12–18 minutes)
1. **Meet-cute / honeymoon montage** (fast, warm, saturated color): the couple meets, falls for each other, moves in together. Shot language: both characters consistently sharing the frame together.
2. **The erosion montage**: small daily frictions accumulate — doors left open, chewing with an open mouth, forgotten toilet paper, unwashed dishes, socks on the floor. No single fight; a slow color drain and a shift to the two characters increasingly framed separately, even in the same room, as the relationship curdles into passive-aggression and silence.
3. **The breaking point**: she packs and heads for the door. As she reaches it, they share a look.
4. **The imagined conversation** (the film's centerpiece): everything unsaid comes out — fear of losing the other person, pride, the real reasons each of them started pulling away. Raw, loud, tearful, honest. It ends with something like "I love you. That's why we can't be together right now" — not a breakup out of not caring, but out of both of them needing to deal with what they're carrying before they can be good for each other.
5. **The rug-pull**: cut back to reality — none of that conversation happened aloud. She's still standing at the door, he's still looking back at her. A beat. She leaves. The door shuts.
6. **Final beat**: hold on the closed door, or on his face, long enough for the audience to feel the tragedy that the honest version of that conversation is one they were both capable of but never had.

### Visual Language (from the source notes — treat these as a formal system to apply consistently, not just mood-board flavor)
- **Framing**: both characters share the frame while things are good; as the relationship deteriorates, cut to separate frames/shots even in shared scenes, visually enacting the split before it happens narratively.
- **Movement vs. stillness**: when the characters are still, something else in frame should move (a candle flame, curtains in a window draft); when the characters move, the environment should hold still — the contrast keeps every shot visually alive without relying on the actors.
- **Color**: bright/warm palette in the opening act, desaturating/fading as the relationship sours; the imagined-conversation sequence could justify a brief return of warmth/saturation (since it's the emotional truth breaking through), collapsing back to cold the instant reality reasserts itself.
- **Directional grammar** (the author's own stated rules — apply as a consistent visual code, not just for this film): left-to-right movement reads as natural/positive; right-to-left as unnatural/negative; stage right as the "good" position, stage left as "bad." Recommend using this to blocking the door scene specifically — e.g., her walking toward the door moves right-to-left as the relationship truly ends, while the imagined-conversation version could reverse the grammar to signal it's the "wrong," unrealized timeline, or vice versa; worth testing on an animatic before committing.

### Craft Notes
- The whole film hinges on the audience not knowing the conversation is imagined until the reveal — avoid any visual "dream-sequence" cue (soft focus, filter) that would tip the twist early; let the visual language (color/framing) shift back to match the "good" era instead, so the reveal reads as bittersweet rather than as a cheap gotcha.
- Research note from the source: read/watch couples-therapy material on common relationship failure patterns (Gottman's "Four Horsemen" — criticism, contempt, defensiveness, stonewalling — is the standard reference and maps directly onto the erosion montage beats already listed) before finalizing dialogue for the imagined-conversation centerpiece, so it reads as specific and true rather than generic.

## Treatment 2: "Quantum Transporter 1.0" (working title)

### Logline
The world celebrates the first successful matter teleporter — until an engineer who helped build it can't stay quiet about what actually happens to the person who steps into the machine.

### Genre
Short sci-fi/techno-thriller, in the vein of *Black Mirror* — a clean, high-concept "one idea, one twist" structure suited to a short runtime.

### Structure (roughly 10–15 minutes)
1. **The unveiling**: a tech-conference keynote, CEO on stage, the Quantum Transporter 1.0 revealed to a cheering crowd — humanity's first successful matter transportation via quantum entanglement/computing. Establish the public euphoria and hype.
2. **The believers**: quick scenes of the personal stakes — a protagonist (recommend anchoring the story on one specific person: a family member of the engineer, a first public volunteer, or the engineer's own decision to use it) excited to use the technology, establishing what teleportation would mean for them personally (reuniting with someone far away, escaping a dead-end circumstance, etc.).
3. **The crack**: an engineer who worked on the technology grows increasingly uneasy — technical detail seeded early (without over-explaining) that hints the "transport" isn't moving matter at all.
4. **The reveal**: the engineer can't stay silent — the horrific truth surfaces. The strongest classic version of this concept (worth deciding deliberately rather than defaulting to it) is that the machine doesn't move a person at all — it destroys the original and creates an identical copy at the destination, meaning every "traveler" has actually died and been replaced, undetectable from the outside. The source notes leave the specific dark secret open; this is the most common and mechanically clean choice for a short, but the author may want something more original.
5. **The cost**: play out the consequence through the one anchor character established in beat 2 — ideally someone we've already watched trust the machine, so the horror lands personally, not just abstractly.
6. **Final image**: recommend ending ambiguous/quiet rather than explanatory — the technology rolling out to the public regardless, unstoppable, the engineer's warning swallowed by the celebration that's already moved on.

### Craft Notes
- This is the least-developed of the three ideas in the source material — essentially a premise and a promise of a twist, no confirmed reveal. Before scripting, the author needs to commit to what the "dark secret" actually is; the destroy-and-recreate teleportation paradox is the well-trodden option (a la Star Trek philosophy debates, or the film *The Prestige*'s Tesla-machine subplot) — strong because it's inherently cinematic and easy to dramatize in a short runtime, but not novel. Alternatives worth considering: the machine only appears to work and the company is hiding total failures/deaths; the machine works but siphons something (memories, a life-span, a soul) with each use; the machine is being used for something other than its stated purpose (surveillance, replacement/infiltration, military).
- Given the short format, keep the science exposition minimal — the emotional throughline (trust, spectacle, hidden cost) matters more than technical plausibility.

## Treatment 3: "Convergence" (working title)

### Logline
A noir detective, a physics professor, a figure skater, and a lawyer find themselves inexplicably occupying the same space at the same time — while, elsewhere, a man who can suddenly perceive every possible version of reality at once is saved from madness by the one person who isn't overwhelmed by it.

### Genre
High-concept short sci-fi/surrealist drama — a genre-blending piece stitching together a multiverse-collision mystery with a parallel, more meditative strand about perception and enlightenment. Comps: *Everything Everywhere All At Once*'s multiverse-collision logic combined with the calm-witness imagery of Buddhist parable/Zen storytelling.

### Structure (roughly 10–15 minutes; two interwoven strands)
**Strand A — The Convergence:**
1. Four strangers from evidently different genres of story — a cynical noir detective, a physics professor, a figure skater, a businessman/lawyer — find themselves suddenly occupying the same space with no explanation.
2. They try to make sense of it together; the professor arrives at the explanation almost immediately once prompted ("Multiple universes colliding"), deliberately undercutting typical mystery-plot pacing — this is a short, not a feature, so the concept should be stated plainly and the film should spend its remaining time on what the characters do with that knowledge, not on withholding it.
3. Recommend developing what happens next explicitly before scripting — the current notes stop right after the reveal. Options: the four have to cooperate to reverse or survive the convergence; the convergence is permanent and they have to decide how to coexist; or the convergence turns out to be connected to Strand B's overwhelmed man, tying the two strands into one causal event.

**Strand B — The Overwhelmed Man:**
4. A man begins perceiving all spectrums, all waves and particles simultaneously — every possible outcome of every moment, faces contorting through a thousand micro-expressions per second, bodies twisting through every possible position at once. Visually, this is the film's most ambitious sequence and should be treated as the emotional/sensory climax, not a throwaway effect shot.
5. He is on the verge of being destroyed by the sheer overload of infinite perception.
6. Salvation arrives as a calm, still figure — a Buddha — who, unlike everyone else rendered in this man's perception as unstable, flickering color (yellow and red), appears fixed, whole, purple and blue, and entirely undisturbed by the chaos. The Buddha's stillness amid infinite possibility is the visual and thematic answer to the man's crisis: peace isn't the absence of chaos, it's the capacity to remain whole within it.

### Craft Notes
- The two strands are not yet connected in the source material — decide early whether Strand A (the four archetypes) and Strand B (the overwhelmed man and the Buddha) are the same event seen from two angles, a cause-and-effect pair, or thematically parallel but narratively separate; a short film has little room for two fully independent plots, so unifying them (e.g., the overwhelmed man's crisis of infinite perception is what's causing/is caused by the universes colliding) is the more producible version.
- The Buddha-as-answer is a strong, simple image — worth protecting from over-explanation. Consider having the Buddha say very little or nothing at all; the visual contrast (stillness/fixed color amid the chaos of shifting color and motion) can carry the resolution without dialogue.
- This is the most visually and conceptually ambitious of the three treatments and would likely require the most VFX/post-production investment relative to runtime — worth sequencing after the other two if the goal is a producible-on-a-budget short film slate.

## Overall Craft Notes
- These three ideas share no characters, setting, or theme — treat this document as a script-idea notebook rather than a single project. If the author wants a cohesive short-film slate, "The Things We Couldn't Say" is the most immediately producible (single location, two actors, no VFX); "Quantum Transporter" is next most producible (single set, one twist, minimal VFX); "Convergence" is the most ambitious and would benefit from being developed last, once the other two establish a visual-language track record.
