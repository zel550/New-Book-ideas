# Girl Kills the Dragon — Full Outline (v2)

## Note on this revision
This replaces the v1 outline (Wren/Ember/Lord Vane version), which was built almost entirely from a two-line source note. The author has since dictated a full, specific plot — a trial frame, a Red-Hood swap, a fellowship of the dragon's own outcasts, Baba Yaga, a betrayal, a golem-in-the-throat escape — and that plot is the real spine of the book from here on. Several beats were dictated with explicit uncertainty ("I'm not sure who," "or something," "I think she dies but I'm not sure"); those are preserved as open decisions below with a recommended default, not silently resolved, since they're the author's own open questions, not gaps in a secondhand source.

## Logline
Sworn to avenge her village against the dragon that once attacked it, a girl swaps places with her best friend on sacrifice night, follows the road into a forest no one returns from, and gathers a fellowship of the dragon's own outcasts to kill it — only to find, from inside the dragon's own throat, that the "monster" might be the one thing in this story that isn't lying to her, right as she's forced to stand trial for what she really did in that forest.

## Genre
YA Fantasy / Fairy-Tale Retelling — Slavic-folklore-inflected (samodivi, Baba Yaga, stone golems), structured around a courtroom/testimony frame in the vein of *Rashomon* — comps: Naomi Novik's *Uprooted* and *Spinning Silver* for the folklore-forward tone and the "the forest itself has rules" worldbuilding, *Damsel* and *The Princess Bride* for the self-aware subversion of the sacrificial-maiden trope, and any Red Riding Hood retelling for the cloak/wolf iconography the plot leans on directly.

## The Frame Device: The Trial
The book opens with the protagonist already on trial, and the bulk of the story is delivered as testimony — witnesses called one by one, each account filling in another piece of what actually happened in the forest, Rashomon-style, with the gaps and contradictions between accounts doing real narrative work (what her parents saw from the road is not what she experienced behind the rock; what the village guards report is not what actually happened at the tree line). The trial isn't just a framing gimmick — per the author's own note, it's built to turn into the story's actual climax: with roughly the final quarter of the book left, the frame breaks. Hidden allies (or the dragon itself, in shapeshifted form — see Open Decision 2 below) are revealed to be present in the courtroom, and the "trial" stops being a retrospective device and becomes the live, forward-moving climax of the book.

### Open Decision 1 — What is she actually on trial for?
The author flagged this as unresolved ("I'm not sure who"). Recommend making the primary charge **releasing the dragon rather than killing it** — she was sent (or sent herself) to kill the village's enemy, and instead the dragon lived and she came back. This is the stronger choice because it pays off the original source note's own framing ("you've heard this story before... but not this one") as a structural joke: the book is titled *Girl Kills the Dragon*, and the whole point is that she doesn't, or not the way anyone expects — the trial exists precisely because "the village's champion let the monster go" is a crime in a world that needed the monster dead. A secondary, compounding charge is worth layering on top: **impersonating the chosen sacrifice** (taking her friend's place was itself against the law of the Choosing, regardless of what happened after) — this gives the early trial chapters a concrete legal hook before the reader even knows what happened in the forest, and lets the "did she kill the dragon" question be the thing that's actually in doubt for most of the book. A death-related charge ("killing someone") can still be folded in as a rumor or a third accusation that turns out to be wrong or twisted — e.g., a village search party or the Wolf's actions caused a death that's being pinned on her — giving the trial a genuine mystery to unravel alongside the folklore adventure, rather than a charge that's just narrative connective tissue.

### Open Decision 2 — Who's hiding in the courtroom?
The author offered two versions. Recommend the **fellowship-in-disguise** version over the shapeshifting-dragon version, for a specific reason: the dragon reveal is a strong twist used once, but the fellowship reveal pays off every relationship built across the whole middle of the book (the goblin, the samodiva, even a redeemed beat for the Wolf if his arc is given one) in a single moment, and it's what naturally motivates the "last 25%" turn the author described — a rescue mission to save the dragon from execution, which only makes emotional sense if the people doing the rescuing are the ones who already learned, alongside the protagonist, that the dragon isn't what the village thinks it is. (The shapeshifting-dragon version isn't wrong, just a different, colder book — worth keeping as a note in case the fellowship version doesn't work in the drafting.)

## Themes
- The gap between the story a witness tells and what actually happened — the trial-frame structure makes this the book's literal architecture, not just a theme
- Vengeance built on an incomplete story — she enters the forest certain of what the dragon is, and the book's whole back half is that certainty coming apart
- Taking someone's place out of love that looks, from the outside, like recklessness — the swap is an act of protection her parents (and the village) initially read as tragedy
- Found family among the discarded — a persecuted goblin, a samodiva defying her own tribe, both cast out by the systems that were supposed to claim them, same as her
- Betrayal from the ally who looks most like you, versus loyalty from the ones who look nothing like you — the Wolf (closest in kind to the "hero" archetype) betrays her; the goblin and the samodiva (both marked as monstrous by others) don't
- The title as a promise the book means to break — *Girl Kills the Dragon* setting up a story that is actually about a girl who doesn't, and has to answer for it

## World
A village bound to a periodic sacrifice after a real, remembered attack — the dragon did burn something, hurt someone, before the story starts; recommend keeping that attack real rather than a total fabrication (unlike v1's "the sacrifice is a total scam" reveal), and instead revealing that the dragon was compelled to attack while bound under Baba Yaga's spell, not acting of its own will. That keeps the village's fear and the protagonist's vengeance legitimate and understandable at the start, while still setting up the reveal that the true villain of the piece is whoever benefits from keeping the dragon bound and the village afraid, not the dragon itself. The forest itself is a hard fairy-tale rule, not just set dressing: no one who enters comes back, the village cannot follow, and there is one path — which the protagonist has to leave, literally and figuratively, once her fellowship starts making choices the road didn't account for.

## Principal Characters
- **The Protagonist** (recommend a name — Slavic-coded to match the world's folklore; a working suggestion: *Yana*) — has trained in secret with a village mentor to fight with sword and spear, driven by a vow of vengeance against the dragon for what it did to her village. Her arc runs from certainty to complication: everything she trained for assumed a simple kill, and the book's real conflict is what she does with that training once the target turns out to be a victim too.
- **The Best Friend** (recommend a name — working suggestion: *Dena*) — the "fair, beautiful maiden" actually chosen by the Choosing, saved by the swap the two of them plotted together behind the rock. Recommend not leaving her offstage for the whole book once the swap happens in Chapter 1 — a strand following what Dena does back in the village while everyone believes her dead or transformed (grief, guilt, possibly investigating what really happened, possibly a witness at the trial) keeps her a real character and not just the inciting device.
- **The Mentor** — the village figure who secretly trained the protagonist to fight, against the village's wishes (recommend deciding whether this person is alive, in hiding, or already dead/a memory by the time the book opens, since it changes whether they can be a trial witness or a source of guidance in the forest).
- **The Goblin** (recommend a name — working suggestion: *Grix*) — one of the dragon's servant-caste, persecuted by his own kind, saved by the protagonist and joining her out of debt that becomes real loyalty. The clever, resourceful member of the fellowship — recommend using him as the one who actually understands how the dragon's binding/curse works, since goblins would know their master's condition better than anyone else in the story.
- **The Samodiva** (recommend a name — working suggestion: *Zora*) — a forest spirit who joins against her own tribe's wishes; recommend giving her tribe's disapproval real stakes (what she risks/loses by defying them) rather than a single line, since it's the clearest mirror of the protagonist's own defiance of her village's expectations.
- **The Wolf** — an anthropomorphic mercenary who joins the fellowship and is later revealed to be working for the dragon, betraying the group before shame drives him into hiding. The Red Riding Hood iconography is already doing thematic work here (the protagonist wears the sacrifice's red cloak; the wolf-figure among her own allies is the one who betrays her, inverting the fairy tale's simple "wolf is the danger" logic) — recommend deciding explicitly whether the Wolf gets a redemption beat before the end (reappearing to help in the climax, e.g., during the courtroom reveal) or stays a cautionary figure, since either is defensible but they lead to different books.
- **Baba Yaga** — the forest witch, whose beauty as she descends her chicken-legged house's stairs is explicitly noted as striking to everyone except the Wolf, who finds her (and all humans) physically repulsive ("naked monkeys") — a good small character beat worth preserving as-is, since it does double duty establishing the Wolf's genuine otherness and Baba Yaga's uncanny nature in one exchange. She reveals the dragon is bound by a spell she "has something to do with" — recommend developing this into a specific, ambiguous moral position (did she bind the dragon to protect the region from something worse, the way v1's dragon-bargain worked, or was she paid/coerced by the same power that benefits from the village's fear?) rather than leaving her motive purely mysterious, since the book needs at least one adult authority figure whose choices the protagonist has to actually reckon with.
- **The Dragon** — attacked the village once, genuinely, but under Baba Yaga's binding spell rather than by choice; recommend a name and a clear sense of what the dragon actually wants (freedom, an end to the compulsion, atonement it's been unable to offer) so that the maw sequence and the eventual rescue land as a real relationship arc between the dragon and the protagonist, not just a mechanical trap-and-escape set piece.
- **The Magistrate / Trial Authority** — recommend a specific antagonist role for the trial frame (a village elder, a regional lord, a priest of whatever institution codified the Choosing) whose real interest is in the dragon staying dead or bound and the "sacrifice" tradition staying intact, since a trial needs a prosecutor with a stake in the outcome, not just an abstract legal process.

## Structure: Four Parts

### Part One — The Swap (Ch. 1–7)
1. Open in the courtroom: the protagonist on trial, charges read (per Open Decision 1 above), the first witness called — establishing the frame and the stakes before any flashback begins.
2. First testimony flashback: the Choosing that selected Dena, the fair maiden, as this year's sacrifice — village dread, false ceremony, the rule that no one returns from the dark forest.
3. The plotted swap, told partly through testimony and partly through the protagonist's own recollection: she and Dena plan it together behind the rock, a genuine act of love and defiance of the Choosing's rules.
4. The procession — the parents' point of view is given real weight here (per the source note): watching "their daughter" stumble, fall behind the rock, rise walking wrong, turning back with the protagonist's blue eyes instead of Dena's, before vanishing into the treeline. Recommend a structural choice to actually shift the narration to the parents for this beat (a witness testimony, told from their seat) so the reader gets the dramatic irony the author described directly, rather than secondhand.
5. Establish the protagonist's vow of vengeance and secret training with the Mentor, woven in as backstory testimony or her own account.
6. Alone on the path into the dark forest — the road's fairy-tale rule established (one path, no return, the village cannot follow).
7. Cut back to the courtroom briefly to reestablish the frame and raise the first real question: something clearly happened that the charges don't fully explain yet.

### Part Two — The Company (Ch. 8–17)
8. First encounter: the persecuted goblin, hunted by his own kind (who were meant to deliver Dena to the dragon); the protagonist saves him.
9. The goblin explains himself — intelligent, no longer willing to serve the dragon — and joins her once she tells him her purpose.
10. The samodiva encountered, and her decision to join against her tribe's wishes — recommend giving this its own real scene of consequence (a confrontation with her tribe, or the cost she's accepted in advance) rather than a quick recruitment beat.
11. The Wolf joins the fellowship — establish him as capable, useful, likable enough that his later betrayal actually costs the reader something.
12. Fellowship dynamics chapter — the found-family warmth the group builds on the road, deliberately contrasted against how alone the protagonist was back in the village.
13. Arrival at Baba Yaga's house on chicken legs — the beauty/repulsion beat exactly as dictated (everyone struck by her beauty except the Wolf, who calls her disgusting, "naked monkeys" and all).
14. Baba Yaga's revelation: the dragon is bound by a spell she "has something to do with" — recommend using this scene to plant the moral ambiguity flagged above rather than resolving it here.
15. Baba Yaga gives the protagonist the miniature stone golem figurine and its activating word, alongside whatever cost or warning comes with such a gift in this world's rules (recommend giving the gift a price, even a small one, so it doesn't read as a pure deus ex machina later).
16. **Open Decision 3 — does Baba Yaga die?** The author flagged this as unresolved. Recommend she does not die on-page here, but that the scene leaves real ambiguity about her fate (she vanishes, or is left facing some consequence off-page) — this preserves her as a possible trial witness or late-book presence without foreclosing a more dramatic version where she's later revealed to have died for her part in binding/unbinding the dragon, which the author can decide once the Baba Yaga scenes are actually drafted.
17. The fellowship leaves Baba Yaga's house and turns onto the final road to the dragon, golem figurine in hand, the group's confidence (misplaced, in the Wolf's case) at its highest point before the betrayal.

### Part Three — The Dragon's Maw (Ch. 18–23)
18. Arrival at the dragon's lair; the confrontation begins as the fellowship prepares to fight.
19. The dragon laughs — the Wolf's betrayal is revealed; he's been working for the dragon the entire journey.
20. Fallout: the fellowship's trust breaks in real time; the Wolf, ashamed, flees/hides rather than pressing his advantage — recommend using his shame here as the seed for whichever redemption/no-redemption choice is made about him later.
21. Cornered and outmatched, the protagonist offers herself to be eaten — a deliberate choice, not a last resort, since she's carrying the golem figurine and already planning the escape (recommend making this clear to the reader even if the fellowship doesn't know her plan, for suspense that's about *how* rather than *whether* she survives).
22. Inside the dragon's throat: she speaks the golem's word; it grows to full size inside the dragon, who heaves and cannot move — the Red Riding Hood analogy made literal (something opens the wolf/dragon's belly from within to let the swallowed girl out).
23. The dragon is forced to spit her out — recommend this be the exact moment the "kill it or free it" question the whole book has been building toward finally has to be answered on the page: with the dragon helpless in front of her, does she still want to kill it, now that she's seen what Baba Yaga told her and felt what it's like inside the thing she swore vengeance on?

### Part Four — The Verdict (Ch. 24–30, the final ~25% per the author's structure)
24. Cut hard back to the courtroom — the testimony has caught all the way up to the present moment, and the trial itself becomes the live climax rather than a framing device.
25. The charge comes to a head: whatever she chose to do with the helpless dragon (per Ch. 23) is exactly what she's being tried for — recommend this be the chapter where Open Decision 1's charges finally land with full weight.
26. The reveal: the fellowship, hidden among the courtroom's attendees, breaks cover at the dramatically appropriate moment (per Open Decision 2) — the goblin, the samodiva, and (if his arc calls for it) even the Wolf, stepping forward to testify or act.
27. The frame breaks entirely — this stops being a trial about the past and becomes a live crisis: the dragon (freed, or about to be executed by the same authority prosecuting her — recommend the latter, since it gives the courtroom scene an actual ticking clock) is in danger, and the fellowship has to act now, in the room, not in testimony.
28. Climactic action in and around the courtroom/execution site — the found family from the forest fighting for the dragon's life in the middle of the very institution that put the protagonist on trial for sparing it.
29. Resolution of the central question the title itself poses: the girl does not kill the dragon — she, and the people she gathered along the way, save it, and the book's real ending is what that costs her with the village, her parents, and the law she's just publicly defied a second time.
30. Final image: recommend closing on the protagonist's relationship to Dena (the friend she swapped places for, whose gratitude or grief needs a real scene by the end) and on what the village's relationship to the dragon becomes now that the truth about the binding spell is public — echoing the source note's own opening promise ("you've heard this story before... but not this one") one last time.

## Craft Notes
- The three explicitly author-flagged open decisions (the trial's charge, who's hiding in the courtroom, whether Baba Yaga dies) are the highest-leverage choices in the whole book — recommend settling all three before drafting begins, since each one reshapes chapters well outside its own scene (the charge shapes every trial chapter; the courtroom reveal shapes how much foreshadowing the fellowship needs earlier; Baba Yaga's fate shapes whether she can return in Part Four).
- The Wolf's arc is the book's sharpest knife and its biggest open question — decide early whether this is a story where trust, once broken, can be earned back (redemption beat in Part Four) or a story where it can't (he stays a cautionary figure, and someone else fills whatever role he might have played in the climax) — both are legitimate, but the rest of the fellowship's characterization should be written differently depending on which is true.
- The parents' POV moment in Ch. 4 is one of the strongest images in the whole dictated plot (a mother and father watching their daughter enter an unreturnable forest, not knowing it isn't her) — worth protecting in the draft rather than summarizing; it's worth a full scene, possibly even its own short chapter from their POV rather than a testimony recap.
- This version keeps v1's "who benefits from the tradition" thread in a new form (Baba Yaga's ambiguous motive, the Magistrate's interest in the dragon staying bound) rather than the original scam-run-by-a-corrupt-steward version — recommend not re-importing Lord Vane or the mountain-network cast from v1 wholesale, since the new plot's antagonist logic (a curse and an institution, not a single scheming villain) is a different and better fit for the trial-frame structure.
