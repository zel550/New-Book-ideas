# The God of Rock — Full Outline

## Logline
A warrior demigod who's spent his life training to meet a legendary father finally finds him tending bar in a forgotten corner of the mortal realm — a washed-up, exiled God of Music too ashamed of the disaster his power once caused to ever play again — and has to decide whether to keep despising him or help him pick the guitar back up before it costs them both everything.

## Genre
Mythic Fantasy Adventure / Action-Comedy. Comps: *Thor: Ragnarok*'s tone, *God of War*'s father-child structure, *School of Rock*'s reluctant-mentor energy, with a pantheon built around music instead of war.

## World / Premise
The Heavens house gods of war, sea, harvest, death — and once housed a God of Music, whose power could shape reality itself through sound. Centuries ago, at the height of his fame, his music went out of control during a performance meant to celebrate the pantheon (a "Concert of the Gods") — the sound tore open something it shouldn't have, killed mortals and minor gods, and nearly unmade a realm. The other gods sealed his signature instrument — a guitar strung with actual starlight/heaven-string — in a vault, stripped him of his title, and cast him down to live among mortals, powerless, nameless, ashamed. He's been hiding in the mortal world ever since, running a dive bar and playing sad, deliberately small songs so nothing ever "goes out of control" again.

## Themes
- Strength redefined — what counts as power, and who gets to decide
- Inherited shame vs. inherited gift: the son has to stop measuring his father against a warrior ideal that was never true
- Fear of your own power as its own kind of prison
- Found respect built through witnessing someone's actual courage, not the myth you wanted them to be
- Redemption that requires doing the terrifying thing again, correctly, not just apologizing for doing it wrong once

## Principal Characters
- **Korrin Ashveil** — the son. Raised by his warrior mother and her war-band after his father vanished; trained his whole life on stories of a legendary, powerful father he never met. Blunt, capable, carries a chip on his shoulder built entirely out of absence.
- **Faelan "Riff" Vane** — the father, former God of Music. Funny, self-deprecating, deeply guilt-ridden; hides real power and real grief under bad bar jokes and a strict personal rule: never play anything with real feeling in it again.
- **Sable** — bartender/mortal friend of Faelan's, the only person who knows who he really is; serves as the audience surrogate who already sees Faelan's worth before Korrin does.
- **General Threnody (the Villain)** — a war-god or fallen general who blames the God of Music's old disaster for the destruction of his own city/army, and has spent decades building a mortal-and-monster army to march on the Heavens in revenge — his war is, thematically, silence vs. sound: he wants music/chaos/emotion purged from the realms entirely.
- **The Heavenly Choir/Council of Gods** — bureaucratic, image-obsessed deities who exiled Faelan more to protect their own reputation than out of real judgment — the story's secondary antagonist force (institutional cowardice vs. Threnody's open violence).

## Structure: Three Acts, 26 Chapters

### Act One — The Bar (Ch. 1–7)
1. Korrin, mid-battle prologue, is overwhelmed by Threnody's forces; a dying mentor/war-band elder gives him the one lead he's always been denied — his father's location.
2. Korrin arrives in the mortal realm's forgotten quarter, finds the dive bar, and finally confronts the man behind the counter — expecting a legend, getting a guy in a stained apron who flinches at loud noises.
3. Faelan denies who he is at first (comic beat), then admits it once Korrin won't leave — the reveal that "God of Music" was real, but that he hasn't played a real note in decades.
4. Korrin's disillusionment: he came for a warrior-father and got a bartender; sharp, wounded arguments establish the emotional core conflict.
5. Threnody's forces track Korrin to the bar; a skirmish forces Faelan to reveal small, reluctant fragments of power (humming a chord that does something small but undeniably godly) — Korrin's first crack in his contempt.
6. Faelan explains, evasively, why he never plays anymore — the Concert disaster, hinted at but not fully shown yet.
7. Korrin, needing allies and information to fight Threnody, reluctantly asks Faelan to come with him — not as a warrior, just as someone who might know how to get back into the Heavens.

### Act Two — On the Road / Building the Band (Ch. 8–19)
8. The road-trip/quest structure begins: father and son, deeply mismatched, forced together toward the Heavens' gate.
9. We meet more of Threnody's motivations in a set-piece battle/flashback — his home realm was destroyed in the fallout of Faelan's disaster; his crusade against "chaos" (music, emotion, unpredictability) is framed sympathetically enough to complicate the villain.
10. Faelan, cornered in danger, uses a sliver more of his power to save Korrin — visibly terrified of his own ability rather than proud of it, subverting Korrin's (and the reader's) expectation of a "power reveal" as triumphant.
11. Sable (introduced earlier or here) rejoins the story, providing Korrin outside context: Faelan didn't just lose his power out of punishment — he's spent decades voluntarily starving himself of it out of guilt, which is its own kind of tragedy Korrin starts to actually see.
12. Full flashback chapter: the Concert of the Gods disaster, shown in full — Faelan playing at the height of his power, the moment of loss of control, the casualties, the other gods' swift, self-protective judgment of him.
13. Korrin realizes the other gods share real blame — they egged Faelan into performing bigger, riskier, more spectacular music for their own glory, then abandoned him the moment it went wrong.
14. Father-son bonding chapter, lighter tone — Faelan teaches Korrin (badly, comically) a little bit about music; Korrin teaches Faelan a little about combat; found-family warmth starts to genuinely build.
15. Threnody's army catches up in force; a real battle where Korrin is badly outmatched, forcing Faelan to choose between his self-imposed silence and his son's life — he plays, just enough, just once, and it visibly costs him (a fresh crack of guilt, a near-loss of control that he barely reins in).
16. Aftermath: Faelan, shaken but alive, admits the guitar (still locked in the Heavens) was never really the problem — his fear of himself is. Korrin starts to reframe what courage looks like.
17. They reach the gate to the Heavens; the Council refuses Faelan entry/reinstatement, more concerned with optics than the coming war — institutional cowardice made concrete.
18. Korrin, furious on his father's behalf for the first time (a full turn from Ch. 1–4's disdain), publicly calls out the Council in front of the other gods.
19. Threnody's full army marches on the Heavens' gate itself; Korrin is captured or gravely wounded holding the line, forcing the story's real climax decision onto Faelan alone.

### Act Three — The Heavenly Rock (Ch. 20–26)
20. Faelan, alone, has to break into the vault holding his own guitar — a heist-style sequence past the same bureaucratic gods who exiled him.
21. Confrontation with the Council: Faelan doesn't ask permission this time — he takes back what's his, on his own terms, while making clear he's not the same reckless god who caused the disaster.
22. Faelan reaches Korrin and Threnody's forces just as the line is about to break; picks up the guitar for the first time in decades.
23. The set piece: "Heavenly Rock" — Faelan playing at full power again, deliberately this time, controlled by hard-won humility rather than ego; visually and structurally the mirror/redemption of the Concert of the Gods disaster.
24. Threnody, confronted directly, gets his moment — not just defeated but heard; Faelan acknowledges the real damage his past caused Threnody's people, offering something the Council never did: accountability instead of exile.
25. Climactic battle resolves through father and son fighting together — Korrin's trained strength and Faelan's reclaimed music combining, thematically uniting "warrior" and "artist" as equally valid forms of power.
26. Resolution: Threnody is defeated or, stronger choice, offered a path to peace once actually heard rather than purged; Faelan is reinstated on his own terms (or chooses to stay mortal-adjacent, running the bar as a place gods and mortals both can hear real music again); Korrin's arc closes on redefining what he actually wanted from a father — not a legend, but someone who shows up.

## Craft Notes
- Keep the humor genuinely funny in Act 1–2 so the Act 3 emotional/action payoff feels earned rather than tonally whiplashed — model the balance on *Thor: Ragnarok* or *Guardians of the Galaxy*.
- Make Threnody's grievance real and specific, not generic villainy — the story's power is in the idea that Faelan's disaster had real victims, and redemption requires facing that, not just reconciling with Korrin.
- Series potential: sequel hooks include other exiled/estranged gods Korrin and Faelan could recruit, and a "world tour" structure for future books built around other pantheon members' domains.
