# Monsieur Zhivakov (The Wolf King) — Full Outline

## Logline
In a city ruled by a secret police whose master turns out to be two people sharing one body, a wolf-man raised to fear his own strength and a girl raised to fear her own powerlessness become the accidental heart of a revolution neither of them meant to lead — one that can only be won by the "monsters" the regime made of everyone, accepting exactly what they've become.

## Genre
Dark Epic Fantasy — political/totalitarian allegory in the vein of *Berserk*, Orwell's *1984*, and Naomi Novik's fairy-tale-structured fantasies, with the moral-collapse texture of Solzhenitsyn's camp writing. Adult content: sexual violence, state terror, and body horror are present in the source material and should be handled the way *Berserk* and serious historical fiction handle atrocity — consequence-focused, never celebratory, always centered on what it costs the people who survive it.

## World
An unnamed city-state ruled by "the Party," officially at peace, actually a totalitarian police state modeled on the historical machinery of the Third Reich, Stalin's USSR, and Beria's secret police (per the source material's explicit references to Himmler, Beria, and Jordan Peterson's lectures on the psychology of totalitarianism). Wolves — a parallel sapient people who can pass as human but carry latent animal strength — are the regime's designated underclass, feared and suppressed the way history has feared and dehumanized minorities; every wolf is raised to suppress their "animal side" to survive among humans. The Party's control runs on Solzhenitsyn's central irony: the labor camps are so under-staffed that prisoners are made to guard themselves, and the terror keeps them too morally compromised to unite against it.

## Themes
- Tyranny as the sum of a thousand small individual moral failures, not one villain's will
- The suppressed self (animal, gendered, traumatized) as a source of power once integrated rather than denied
- Stories as a legitimate form of truth — a myth people believe in is not "a lie" once it changes what they do
- The complicity trap: systems that survive by making their victims guard, betray, and brutalize each other
- Whether evil is a single figure to be destroyed or a condition every person carries and must own

## Principal Characters
- **Zhivakov** (drafted in later English-language notes as "Bernard Géroux" — treat as one character; recommend settling on a single final name before drafting) — a wolf, raised by an abusive stepfather, trained by a sword-master in an aikido-inflected philosophy: strength is never for domination. Secretly wants to be a bounty hunter — chasing a life that *looks* good rather than one that *feels* good, the book's opening character flaw. Kills his abusive stepfather early in the book; his arc is learning to integrate his suppressed "animal side" without being consumed by it.
- **The Girl** (unnamed in source; recommend a name, e.g. **Yara**) — loses her parents to a public execution after they try to hide her from the Villain's carriage; is raped, sworn into the Party, then sent to a labor camp, where she survives, is betrayed by friends, and hardens into a fighter. Wants Zhivakov's raw power so she can punish the people who hurt her; her arc mirrors his in reverse — powerless learning to claim strength, where he is powerful learning to control it.
- **The Villain** — the Party's ruler; publicly a man, secretly one body split into two identities: "the Brother" (violent, direct, the public tyrant) and "the Woman" (unmasked, manipulative, governs through myth-making and psychological control rather than force). The dissociation is not revealed to the reader as a twist until the climax.
- **The Woman** (the Villain's alternate identity) — sees that fear alone can't hold the city together, so she invents the legend of the Wolf King and quietly maneuvers Zhivakov into fulfilling it, the way she also grooms the Girl into becoming a folk hero to control her too. Modeled explicitly (per the source notes) on Griffith from *Berserk* — sympathetic, intelligent, willing to sacrifice anyone for her vision of order.
- **The Sword-Master** — Zhivakov's teacher; his home is burned and his people slaughtered by Party enforcers midway through the book, the event that first breaks Zhivakov's control.
- **The Wild Wolf Tribe and their Leader** — free wolves living outside the city who never learned to suppress their animal side; Zhivakov must defeat their leader in unarmed, wolf-to-wolf combat to be named Wolf King and win their aid for the uprising.
- **The Dragon** — enslaved beneath the castle, an archetypal predator the Villain has tortured into starved submission, exactly as he has tortured the city and the wolves; freed at the climax as the story's central image of what suppression does to power, and what happens when it's finally released.
- **The Good Youth** — a minor character who is entirely, uncompromisingly good, and dies precisely because goodness cannot survive unmodified in the camp/city system; his death is the story's proof that good still exists and is worth fighting for, even though it isn't enough on its own to win.

## Structure: Three Acts, 32 Chapters

### Act One — Two Origins (Ch. 1–10)
1. Cold open, intercut: Zhivakov training under the Sword-Master, learning a fighting philosophy built on restraint — power is never to be used for domination.
2. The Girl's family, out past curfew, encounters the Villain's night carriage; papers are checked; they're ordered to bring her to the palace at 10 a.m.
3. The family decides to hide her instead and hopes to talk their way out of it.
4. The Villain's police raid the apartment; the Villain's chilling, courteous menace as he searches the home and finds the hiding place anyway.
5. Public execution of the Girl's parents, staged as a forced ideological ritual in which the Villain manipulates the Girl into publicly renouncing and cursing them before the shooting — the book's first full demonstration of how the regime manufactures complicity in its victims, not just obedience.
6. That night, the Villain rapes the Girl; the Woman (his alternate identity) appears afterward to "comfort" her with the framing that suffering is simply the burden of being a woman — planting the seed of the Girl's core wound: hatred of her own perceived powerlessness.
7. The Girl is sent to a labor camp the next morning.
8. Parallel to this: Zhivakov's own home life — an abusive stepfather who has beaten him, his brother, and their mother his whole life.
9. Enforcers burn down the Sword-Master's home and slaughter people close to Zhivakov while he and his teacher are away; Zhivakov loses control for the first time, killing the attackers in a full animal rage. The Sword-Master, grieving, tells him he will be hunted for it the rest of his life.
10. Zhivakov returns home and kills his stepfather — establishing his own version of the Girl's core wound: fear of his own power and what it makes him capable of.

### Act Two — The Camp and the City (Ch. 11–22)
11. Camp life: the labor camp is run, per Solzhenitsyn's irony, by its own prisoners, understaffed by the Party on purpose — creating a system where prisoners must guard, ration, and sometimes sacrifice each other to survive.
12. The Girl is left to fend for herself; her survival instinct kicks in (a fight over food, a threat from other prisoners) and she chooses to fight rather than die.
13. She is betrayed by close camp friends — an informant network runs through the prisoners themselves — hardening her resolve toward one goal: killing the Villain and destroying his regime.
14. Zhivakov, having fled after killing his stepfather, is eventually captured (or turns himself toward the same camp system through misfortune) and ends up in the same camp, fighting in enforced gladiatorial matches against guards, where his suppressed strength draws dangerous attention.
15. The Woman visits the camp, having noticed Zhivakov's raw, barely-contained ferocity in the fights; she offers him a way out — protection and purpose, in exchange for loyalty and the use of his strength to help her "tame the wolves." He refuses: better to die a free wolf than live as her dog.
16. The Woman turns to the Girl instead, cultivating her into a symbol/hero within the camp's resistance network, intending to use her as a tool the same way she's tried to use Zhivakov — the mirrored manipulation of both leads becomes visible to the reader before either lead sees it in the other.
17. Zhivakov and the Girl meet properly inside the camp; her fire and his restraint recognize each other. She tells him the story already spreading among the wolves and prisoners — that of the "Wolf King," a myth the Woman quietly engineered and seeded, with Zhivakov (unknowingly) cast as its subject.
18. A secret resistance meeting is being watched; Zhivakov, hunting the informant, loses himself to his animal senses and kills the spy in a full predatory frenzy — only to discover afterward that the spy was a child. This atrocity, committed by our protagonist, is the book's hardest scene and must sit with him (and the reader) as real, unredeemed weight, not a shrug.
19. Amid the horror, one grace note: the "flying whale" set piece — a once-a-year magical migration of a whale-like creature trailing thousands of light-feeding fireflies over the city, briefly uniting everyone (guards and prisoners, wolves and humans) in wonder — shot down by Party guards for a state banquet, the story's clearest single image of the regime destroying beauty for its own appetite.
20. Escape/uprising planning begins in earnest; Zhivakov and the Girl, now allied, start organizing the camp's latent resistance, aided by the Sword-Master's aikido-derived philosophy of restrained strength as their operating code — contrasted against the brutal survival tactics (informing, stealing food, sacrificing the weak) most prisoners have had to adopt to live this long.
21. Zhivakov leaves the camp to seek the Wild Wolf Tribe beyond the city, hoping to recruit them to the uprising.
22. He earns their aid by defeating their leader in unarmed, wolf-to-wolf combat, fully surrendering to his animal side under controlled will for the first time rather than losing himself to it — he is named Wolf King, and leads the tribe back toward the city.

### Act Three — The Wolf King (Ch. 23–32)
23. The wild wolves descend on the city and join the uprising; a wild wolf helps a "tamed," suppressed wolf back to its feet mid-battle — the book's central symbolic image of the animal/self alignment Zhivakov (and every wolf in the city) has been fighting toward.
24. Quiet chapter: Zhivakov and the Girl talk about what they each fear most — her, being powerless again; him, losing control permanently and becoming the caged, feral wolf he once saw as a child. Their outsider kinship (different in kind, alike in being despised) deepens without becoming a tidy romance — the source material is explicit that they never end up together, and their bond should read as chosen-family/comradeship, not a love-plot resolution.
25. The Girl and Zhivakov find the Dragon chained beneath the castle; it speaks, reveals crucial intelligence that will help topple the Villain, and asks in exchange to be freed once he falls — an uneasy bargain, since a hungry ancient predator freed in a broken city is its own risk.
26. The resistance moves on the castle; the Woman appears to help broker the wolves' surrender/alliance for the final assault — but it's a trap, engineered to secure the wolves' total submission through the appearance of an ally's betrayal.
27. The Villain reveals his identity is split from the Woman's — the twist the reader has been circling all book: brother and sister sharing one body, alternating masked, each pursuing a different theory of control (force vs. myth). Zhivakov, already exhausted from putting down a hundred soldiers and their captain in the assault (a feat that gives the whole uprising hope), duels the masked "sister," loses the fight, but manages to tear off the mask.
28. The mask comes off to reveal the Villain, not a separate sister — dissociative reveal complete. He threatens to butcher Zhivakov and everyone who ever speaks his name. Zhivakov, spent, laughs and says he won't win — because "she" does; the Girl, who has fought her own way into the room, attacks and defeats the Villain without killing him.
29. The confrontation scene (source material's centerpiece): the Villain recognizes the Girl as the child he raped and destroyed years earlier; demands she say she remembers before he'll accept death; instead she makes him say it. He delivers his defense — that he only revealed what was already inside the citizens, that the city was already full of monsters, that killing him changes nothing because evil will always find a new face. The Girl doesn't refute it — she agrees the city is full of monsters, including herself and Zhivakov, and that this — not denial of what they've become — is the only way to actually save it.
30. Rather than executing him herself, the Girl drags him to the balcony in front of the rebels who've broken into the castle, tells the crowd the hard truth — that they are monsters now, and only by owning that, not pretending innocence, can they save their city — then throws the Villain to the crowd, who tear him apart. This is deliberately not a clean, redemptive ending; it's the book's thesis made literal.
31. Simultaneously, the Villain's last order — releasing the starved, tortured Dragon on his enemies — backfires: freed from its magical chains by the Girl and Zhivakov as promised, the Dragon devours the Villain's remaining loyalists (or, if the timeline is adjusted, the Villain himself before the balcony scene — recommend keeping the balcony scene as the emotional/thematic climax and the Dragon's release as the physical climax immediately preceding it), then climbs the castle and lets out a final roar, its scarred, starved body a visible metaphor for what the regime has done to the whole city — before flying off, promising the survivors a future favor.
32. Coda: the Good Youth's earlier death (recommend placing this beat in Act Two, around Ch. 18–19, as the event that hardens the group's resolve rather than only being referenced) is remembered here as proof that unmodified goodness existed and mattered, even though it couldn't survive the system alone; Zhivakov, having stopped believing the Wolf King story was "real," is told by the Girl that it doesn't matter where a story comes from — enough people believing it makes it true. He accepts the title, not as myth, but as responsibility. Final image: Zhivakov and the Girl, allies and equals, not lovers, parting ways to rebuild — him among the wolves, her at the head of whatever government replaces the Party — each carrying forward what the other taught them.

## Craft Notes
- This material is dense with atrocity (sexual violence, execution, forced complicity, animal-rage killing of a child). None of it should be staged for shock; every instance should exist to interrogate how ordinary people and systems produce monsters, per the book's explicit thesis, and should be written with the same restraint the source notes ask of Zhivakov's own fighting philosophy.
- Decide early whether the book is titled/narrated around "Zhivakov" (Eastern European register, matching the totalitarian-state allegory) or "Bernard Géroux" (the later English draft's naming) — the two names appear to represent an in-progress rename rather than two characters; pick one before drafting prose.
- The Girl needs a settled name before drafting (recommend Yara, used above) — the source notes never name her.
- Consider trimming scope for a debut manuscript: the Wild Wolf Tribe recruitment, the Dragon subplot, and the Woman/Villain political maneuvering are each big enough to be their own book; a duology (Book 1: camp/uprising through the Wolf King naming; Book 2: the siege of the castle and the Villain's fall) may serve the material better than one very long novel.
